Decision sheet

Final state after four rounds: six experts, a critic, two red-team reviewers, the panel's rebuttal, a family-business coach, and three buyer walkthroughs. Everything below this sheet is the reasoning.

Decided

Eight calls

  • Name: With Love by Iris is the brand and site. Wholesome Living stays as the bath and body line. Wordmark is plain "Iris" in the label serif; the watercolor system goes on everything.
  • Categories: Bath & Body, Candles & Prayer, Custom Sewing, Gifts, plus Our Story. "For Him" is a tag.
  • Catalog: 12 SKUs. Five shampoo scents plus one seasonal, two men's balms, three candle verses, one Paw-Fection two-pack. Lip gloss, face line, muscle balm, samplers, gift sets, Find Me At are cut.
  • Prices: retail at least 4x materials, wholesale 2x, enforced by the catalog. Bars $12 (3 for $32), balm $18, candles $22 to $24, Paw-Fection $10 booth, $6 wholesale at a 12-unit prepaid minimum.
  • Bears: one a month at $195, 50% deposit by draft order, inquiry only, form closes when booked. Five paid deposits on a waiting list is the year-two go or no-go.
  • Channels: the site, six fairs a year that clear $500 gross and $30 an hour after materials, Facebook, funeral home and hospice brochures, the vet on prepaid wholesale. Etsy, consignment, paid ads, Facebook Shop are out for year one.
  • Hours: she already puts in about 20 a week because it is her hobby, so the number is not the problem, the split is. Target: 8 making core products, 3 bears, 3 packing, 2 content, 1 admin, and 3 hours of Workbench play that is hers, no questions asked. Justin logs the split for four weeks. She is a full-time caregiver, so any week can drop to zero and the plan has to survive that.
  • New ideas: Workbench, then a 30-day booth trial of 10 units with a legal label, then the catalog. No "for sale" post before ten strangers paid. One fixed monthly transfer funds supplies; disability money never buys inventory directly.
  • Audience: the Facebook Page lives in a Business Manager Justin owns, Iris is an editor, and the email list is the asset, because she deletes her personal profile yearly. Three of four posts are about core products.
  • Site v1: catalog, guest checkout with Bethel pickup and $6 flat rate (free over $45) with wallets, bear inquiry with deposit, three transactional emails, day-55 reorder reminder. Bundles, subscriptions, accounts, /booth deferred.

The model in one paragraph

Iris sells comfort to one buyer: a woman aged 45 to 70 in Clermont County and her circle, who buys for family and church friends. Three lines serve that buyer. Wholesome Living bath and body brings her back every six weeks. Scripture beeswax candles bring in new people as gifts. Memory bears are the flagship: high price, no competition at the bazaar, referred by funeral homes and hospices, found on Etsy and Google by people in grief. Craft fairs and Facebook find customers; the website keeps them. Etsy is a five-listing billboard for the bears and quilts, never a store.

Revised after adversarial review: the red team cut this further. Etsy is out, consignment is out, the catalog is capped at 12 SKUs, bears are capped at one a month at $195, and the plan assumes 10 hours a week, not 25. The rules and revised calls at the end win over anything above them.

Positioning line from the marketing panelist: "Handmade in Bethel, Ohio, with scripture and love: comfort for your body, your home, and your memories."

Name

With Love by Iris is the house brand and site. Wholesome Living stays as the bath and body line name, so nothing printed is wasted. "Wholesome Living" cannot carry a grief keepsake; "With Love by Iris" fits a bear tag, a candle lid, and a shampoo wrap. Keep the "ir!s" stylization on labels; use plain "Iris" in URLs, email, and the Facebook page name so people can search it.

Pricing rule

Retail at 4x materials, wholesale at 2x. If 4x feels high, the materials cost is wrong or the product is. Bars $12 (3 for $32), balm $18, candle $22 to $24, paw balm $10 at the booth. She is not taking a loss on the paw balm at $10 in person; she only loses when she subsidizes a mailer for one tube.

The vet: the panel split between consignment and wholesale. Call: consignment first (the vet pays nothing up front, she keeps 60 to 70% of $10, restocks monthly). Once it sells through twice, switch to wholesale at $6 with a 6-unit minimum so she is paid on delivery. Same template for groomers, the feed store, and boutiques.

Read it honestly

If she counts only the 480 hours the sales themselves take, that is $14.70 an hour. If she works the full 25-hour week the finance panelist budgets (about 1,300 hours), it is $5.44 an hour, and paying herself $20 an hour would show a $2,500 loss. Year one is a paid hobby that covers its costs. Year two, at 30 online orders a month and 12 bears, clears $20 an hour plus profit. That is a normal solo-maker curve, and it beats the same year on Etsy by roughly $1,500 in fees. The lever that moves the number is bears, not more scents.

Stock, cash, and the making week

  • One product family per making day, one making day a week. Batch 24 to 48 bars, 12 to 24 balms, 12 candles. Hold 4 to 6 weeks of stock per item; remake at 25% remaining, and set the site's low-stock alert to match.
  • Launch stock: about 60 bars, 24 candles, 20 paw balms, 10 of each balm. More than that is cash on a shelf.
  • Lot codes: date plus a product letter on every batch, logged in a notebook. This is her MoCRA record and her recall plan.
  • Cash rule: keep one month of costs, about $600, in the business account. Never spend fair cash before it is deposited and logged on Friday.
  • Weekly hours, 25: production 10, fairs 4 on average, packing 3 (Monday and Thursday only), content 3 (one photo session, one long post), admin 1 (Friday).

Three sticky-note metrics

  1. Revenue per fair hour, including drive and setup. Under $30 an hour, drop that fair.
  2. Online orders this month vs last. If it is not rising, the content plan is failing, not the site.
  3. Cash in the business account on Friday. Two drops in a row, stop buying supplies.

Labels she must fix

  • Every cosmetic (bars, balms, gloss, mask, scrub): identity statement ("Shampoo Bar"), net weight in oz and grams on the front, full INCI ingredient list in descending order (she needs the base supplier's INCI list, "shampoo base" does not count), business name and address, and a phone or email for adverse-event reports. The round watercolor wrap will not hold all of it; add a small back label or hang tag.
  • MoCRA: under $1M in sales she is exempt from FDA registration, listing, and GMP. She still keeps a batch log with lot codes and must report a serious adverse event within 15 business days.
  • Claims: "cooling mint muscle balm" is fine, "relieves sore muscles" is a drug claim. Mask cannot "detox." Gloss cannot "heal." Paw balm ceiling is "moisturizes, lick-safe ingredients"; "heals cracked pads" is a veterinary drug claim. Beef tallow goes rancid, so date the tubes.
  • Candles: ASTM F2058 caution text on the tin bottom (burn within sight, keep away from things that catch fire, keep away from children and pets, trim wick to 1/4 inch, 3 to 4 hour max burn).

Etsy rules that matter

Etsy prohibits steering buyers off-platform in listings, messages, or the shop announcement. The About page "around the web" links are allowed. Inside the box is hers: a kraft thank-you card with the site, the Facebook page, a 10% code for the next order, and a photo of the whole line, since the bear buyer never saw the candles. Etsy also collects and remits Ohio sales tax on those orders for her.

The site: architecture and the five flows to build first

Assume a phone screen for everything. Small beauty stores convert around 1.2 to 1.9% on mobile with 65 to 75% of traffic there; Apple Pay and Google Pay are the single biggest lift. The UX panelist collapsed the proposed seven-category tree, which mixed audience, type, and craft, into four.

Shop Bath & Body (the Wholesome Living line: bars, balms, gloss, later mask/scrub) For Him (a landing page and tag, not a category) Candles & Prayer (scripture beeswax candles, devotional intent) Custom Sewing (memory bears, quilts; inquiry-driven) Gifts (scent sampler, gift sets, paw balm two-pack) Our Story Find Me At (upcoming fairs, booth number, pickup window)

Mobile nav: logo, Shop, Our Story, cart. Nothing else.

When an order comes in, for Iris

Your phone and Justin's get a text and an email. You do not have to do anything that day. Pack on Monday and Thursday. Print the label on Pirate Ship (free, cheaper USPS rates), tape it to a padded mailer (buy 50 at a time, about 50 cents each), and schedule a free USPS carrier pickup from the porch on the Pirate Ship screen. No trip to the post office. Pickup orders: the site texts the customer your porch window; you set it once. The site promises "ships within 3 business days," which Monday and Thursday packing always meets.

Summer: balms and lip gloss soften above 85°F in a mailbox. June through August, ship them in a padded mailer with a "may soften in heat, cool before opening" card, or steer locals to pickup. Candles and bars do not care.

Photos: Justin shoots the whole batch once, on the same board, in the same afternoon light, with a phone. One session per product family. Iris shoots the workbench and process shots for Facebook; those do not need to be pretty.

Justin's time: the five build steps are roughly 40 to 60 hours before launch, then an hour or two a week for a month. Saturday fixes go through a text; nothing on the site needs same-day attention except a payment failure, which Stripe emails him.

Retention: the bars are the business

A shampoo bar lasts 4 to 6 weeks. Beard balm 6 to 8. Beauty repeat-purchase rates run 25 to 40%, with half of second orders inside 30 days. Candles and bears bring new people; bars keep them.

  • Three plain-text emails in her voice: confirmation; shipped or ready-for-pickup with the how-to-use card; day 14 "How's the lather?" asking for a review that renders on the product page, no third-party widget.
  • Reorder reminder at day 55 for a single bar, day 100 for a sampler, one-tap link prefilled with the last scent.
  • Bundles: Scent Sampler (3 half bars, choose scents), Gift Set (bar plus candle in the honeycomb bag), Men's Set (balm plus candle).
  • Free shipping at $45. Pushes the two-bar order to three bars and a gloss, and covers the label.
  • One loyalty mechanic she can honor: "Bring a friend." Friend gets 10% off; referrer gets a handwritten note and a free lip gloss in the next order.
  • Reorder card in every bag at the booth, business-card size, kraft, label art on the front, QR on the back.

Adversarial review

Justin asked for critics, not cheerleaders: "incredible technical skill but bad taste and business direction sometimes." Two reviewers were briefed to attack. A boutique buyer judged the actual product photos. A skeptical investor attacked the plan and the premise. The original panel then had to concede or rebut each point; the results are folded into the rules and revised calls below.

The taste critic, from the photos

Sellable as-is

This is the brand

  • Shampoo bar labels. Watercolor botanical wreaths with the scent illustrated. Best design decision in the line. Pull everything else toward it.
  • The corduroy postal bear. Right proportions, a real snout, close-set bead eyes, one fabric, one uniform. Character bears in this register (mail carrier, nurse, farmer) are a product line, not a one-off.
  • Memory bear construction and the birth-stat clouds on the baby quilt. Skill is not the problem.
Reads as hobby-craft

Three products, three brands

  • Candle lids: matte black tins are good; the orange clipart flame-and-cross sticker belongs on a VBS handout and cheapens pure beeswax. Cream label, verse reference in the label serif, one thin gold line.
  • The "ir!s" wordmark reads as a 2009 screen name. Set "Iris" in the label serif and let the watercolor carry the personality.
  • Lip gloss: green tube, green label, "Natural alternative" down the side looks like an MLM starter roller. Amber tubes with a small botanical label, or keep it off the site.
  • Bee bags: three different prints, one with gnomes. Party favor, not gift. Plain kraft or cream with one sticker.
  • Kraft wraps: crumpled pleats show in every photo. Crisp seam underneath, or a paper band.
  • Puns work on Facebook where regulars know her. On a product page a stranger reads "RUFF day" as a Cricut vinyl shop. Cut them from titles, one per description at most.
The memory bear rule

A stranger would buy the pink floral bear with the tulle skirt, the tan bear family in shirts, and the corduroy bear. Only a family member loves the plaid bears with a photo of the deceased ironed onto the chest, the purple bear with handwriting on its feet, and the cheerleader with mismatched eyes and drawn eyebrows. The rule: the body is one calm fabric and the memory is worn as clothing or a single accent. When the loud fabric becomes the whole body, or a face is printed on the belly, it is a memorial object, not a bear. Two matched small dark eyes, a stitched nose, one accessory. This is the Reds-print problem, stated as a standard she can hear.

Where her instinct beats the panel: the scripture candles. Unscented pure beeswax, a verse per tin, "light it while you pray." Designers will want to soften the faith or scent the wax. Wrong. Devotional buyers are loyal, underserved by tasteful products, and gift in multiples. Only the sticker changes.

Reshoot, phone only: bars top-down on a cream linen napkin by a window plus one lathered hand; one lit candle in a dim room after the label redesign; the postal bear seated on a white stool, eye level; the quilt flat on white sheets from directly above, then one cloud close-up. Portrait mode off, tap to focus, no flash, edit brightness only. Nothing shot on sequin fabric or the pegboard goes live.

The business skeptic, on the plan

  • Hobby with a website. The panel's own year is $14k revenue and $7k before owner pay on about 1,300 hours: $5.44 an hour. A business pays her $20 an hour and clears something. That needs roughly $35k: 30 fairs at $500, 40 online orders a month, 24 bears. Every input is 2x to 4x today. Under 15% odds in 12 months.
  • Five ways it dies: SKU creep (ten scents, face products, a dog balm, a postal bear in one season, and nobody assigned to enforce the cut); Justin as the single point of failure; fairs that already sit at the $30-an-hour kill line before materials; bears do not batch, and 24 of them is 96 hours with a dead husband's shirts on the table; launch stock of about $900 in materials against a $600 cash reserve.
  • Where the panel went soft: a free bear for a funeral director is four hours and $16 per partner before any referral (give a brochure); consignment and wholesale for the same vet is two policies for one customer, and consignment is unpaid inventory in someone else's building; five Etsy listings with no sales history and ads off will not surface in search, so that is a listing fee, not a billboard; "bears are the real business" and "bars are the business" appear in the same report.
  • Survivable on 10 hours a week: six fairs a year that already clear $500, one process post a week, five bars, two balms, candles, and a bear inquiry form capped at one bear a month at $195. Kill Etsy, consignment, gift sets, samplers, the vet, the face line, the postal bear, and the Find Me At page. Justin builds steps 1, 3, and 4 only.
Skeptic's verdict

Fix it, do not fund it. Spend the $700 on legal and nothing else until she holds the SKU cap for 90 days. The one thing that changes the call: five paid bear deposits at $195 on a waiting list. Then it is a business, and the bars are the gift-shop sideline they always were.

Saying it to her

A family-business coach read the rules and flagged the three that will land as judgment: the 12-SKU cap (reads as "half your work is not good enough"), the price floor (reads as "you may not be generous"), and curated bears (Justin choosing which of her sewing is good enough to show). Rule 8, the quarterly kill, is painless at launch and explosive at the first review.

Each rule, in words he can say

Site standards, not verdicts

  1. "Anything on the site needs the watercolor label and the ingredient panel the law expects, so the site only lists what already has both."
  2. "Online shoppers can't pick it up, so every listing gets the same window-light photo setup. I shoot, you make."
  3. "Twelve is a shelf size, not a verdict. The site shows twelve; the booth shows everything."
  4. "The site has a price floor built in, four times materials, so the beeswax pays for itself. Paw-Fection can be a gift; the site just can't be where you give it."
  5. "The bears page shows the four that photograph best and takes a deposit through the form, because custom work gets paid before you cut fabric."
  6. "The form closes at one bear a month so no family waits and you never sew grief on a deadline."
  7. "Supplies come off the business card, and the card keeps $600 back. Every shop has a rainy-day rule."
  8. "Each quarter I pull what sold. Under ten in ninety days rotates off to make room. It can always come back."
The market-only lane

"Not on the site" never means "no"

  • Booth table: all ten scents, the postal bear, the Reds bears, samplers, muscle balm. The fair is her stage; the site is the shop window. Say it in those words.
  • Facebook: her voice, her puns, her long posts, untouched. Site copy is his; Facebook is hers.
  • A "From the Workbench" page: phone photos allowed, no cart, no prices, "ask at the booth or message Iris." New ideas debut here. Ten inquiries earns a catalog slot, so the SKU cap becomes a promotion path instead of a wall.
  • Vet lane: Paw-Fection wholesale is her deal with the vet, not a site product. Samples she hands out are hers to give.
The one conversation before launch

"Mom, the site is your shop window, not your whole workshop. Everything you make still gets made."
"The window holds twelve things. The booth and Facebook hold all of it."
"I'll run the boring parts: labels, photos, prices, the quarterly numbers. That's my job so it isn't yours."

Never say: "that doesn't sell" (say "hasn't sold online yet"); "MLM," "homemade-looking," "cheap," "clutter"; "the panel said" or "the numbers say" as a shield, since he owns the rules; "you can't" (the site can't, she can); "ten is too many" (say "twelve slots"); "without appeal," which stays in his notes.

The gate between making and selling

Two facts Justin added after the panel closed. She goes from drawing board to product line to Facebook "for sale" post in about a week. And the money funding this is a disability increase for the husband she cares for full time. Both change the rules: her hours are not hers to schedule, and the capital is not the business's to risk. She also works about 20 hours a week on this by choice. That is not a problem to cut; it is the reason a play slot has to exist inside the plan, so the hobby energy goes to the Workbench and not into the catalog.

Three stages, one week each minimum

Nothing skips a stage

  1. Workbench (private). Make it, use it herself for a week, photograph it for the Workbench page. Process posts on Facebook are fine here: "trying a new balm" is content, not a listing. No price, no "message me to buy."
  2. Trial (30 days, 10 units). One small batch. Sell at the booth or to friends only, with a full legal label on every unit. Log what sold, what came back, what people said. No site listing, no "now available" post, no reorder of supplies.
  3. Catalog. Ten units sold in 30 days at 4x materials, legal label, window-light photos, and a retired slot if the twelve are full. Then it gets a page and a for-sale post.
The capital rule

Disability money is not working capital

  • One fixed transfer a month into the business account (Justin and Iris pick the number; $150 is plenty at this scale). That is the only money that ever buys supplies. Nothing comes from the household account directly.
  • Supplies for a Workbench-stage idea are capped at $40 and must fit inside that month's transfer. A trial batch is capped at $100. Catalog restocks come from sales revenue only.
  • Fair fees are prepaid from the business account the month before, so a fair never competes with a supply order.
  • If the business account cannot fund it, the idea waits a month. It does not come out of caregiving money, ever.
Why the week matters

A one-week cycle means every idea gets label costs, supply orders, a photo, and a public promise before anyone has paid for one. The trial stage moves the public promise to after ten strangers paid. It also protects her: a "now available" post she cannot fulfill because a caregiving week ate the batch is the fastest way to lose Facebook trust. Process posts carry no promise, so those stay unlimited. Justin holds the stage gate the same way he holds the SKU cap: through what gets a page.

Say it as: "New things get a month at the booth first. If ten people buy it there, it earns a page. That way nobody's waiting on a post when you have a hard week with Dad."

Two more facts, two more rules

She always markets the new thing

Three of four posts are core

  • The bars, balms, and candles pay the bills, and they are the products nobody has heard of yet. New ideas get the attention; core products get the sales. The ratio inverts that: in any week, three posts are about catalog products, one may be Workbench.
  • The Monday process post is always a core product: a batch of bars, a candle pour, a bear in progress. "Trying something new" is the Wednesday or Friday slot at most, never the weekly lead.
  • Justin drafts the four weekly posts on Sunday from her photos and her words. She can rewrite them. She cannot swap a core post for a new-product post without a Workbench-page inquiry count to point at.
  • Every for-sale post links to a product page, which only core products have. The site enforces the ratio by having nothing to link a new idea to.
She deletes her Facebook once a year

The audience cannot live on her profile

  • True deletion takes the friends list, the post history, and any Page she is the sole admin of. Everything the marketing panel built on "post from her personal profile too" is withdrawn.
  • The Page is created under a Meta Business Manager that Justin owns, with Iris as an editor, never the only admin. If her profile goes, the Page, its followers, and its post history stay.
  • The email list is the real audience asset because nobody can delete it in a bad week. Every booth signup, every checkout, every Page follower gets asked for an email within the first month.
  • Long process posts are written on the site's Workbench or story page first and shared to the Page, so the writing survives the account.
  • When she deletes: nothing on the business side changes, and she can come back as an editor whenever she wants. Say that to her before launch so the yearly reset is not a business event.

Words in this report, for Iris

  • SKU: one thing you sell at one price. Lavender bar and Vanilla bar are two SKUs.
  • INCI list: the official ingredient names the label must use ("Butyrospermum Parkii (Shea) Butter"). Your base supplier has this list.
  • Product page: the page for one item, with photos, price, and the Add to cart button.
  • Wallets: Apple Pay and Google Pay. The customer taps once instead of typing a card number.
  • Draft order: an invoice Justin creates in the admin and emails as a pay link. Used for bear deposits.
  • Inventory kit: a bundle whose parts come out of stock when it sells, so a Sampler subtracts three half bars.
  • Consignment: the shop pays you only after it sells. Wholesale: the shop pays you up front at a lower price.
